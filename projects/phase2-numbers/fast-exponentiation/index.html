    How it works

    To compute b^e you don't need e−1 multiplications. Write e in binary and scan its digits from the most significant down. Keep a running result: at every digit, square it; and whenever the digit is a 1, also multiply by the base. When the digits run out, the result is b^e. That's about log₂(e) squarings plus one multiply per 1-bit — roughly 2·log₂(e) operations, versus e−1.

    For 2¹⁰⁰⁰ that's 14 multiplications instead of 999. The gap only widens: the naïve cost grows with the exponent itself, the fast cost with its number of digits.

    Modular exponentiation is the same ladder with one extra move: reduce mod m after every square and every multiply. The numbers never grow past m², so 7^1000000 mod 13 is instant even though 7^1000000 has over 800,000 digits. This is exactly how public-key cryptography raises numbers to enormous secret exponents without ever forming the enormous number.

    Inputs: whole numbers of any size (BigInt). The base may be negative; the exponent must be zero or positive; the modulus, if given, must be greater than 1.
