Collatz Conjecture

Pick any whole number. If it's even, halve it; if it's odd, triple it and add one. Repeat. The conjecture — open since 1937 and still unproven — is that you always crash back down to 1. Type a number and watch the hailstone sequence climb and fall.

Try:

Reaches 1 in — steps

    Trajectory — every value on the way down

    The sequence — the numbers themselves

    Record hunt — who flies longest and highest?

    Sweep every start from 1 up to a ceiling and find the champions: the number whose orbit takes the most steps, and the one that climbs to the highest altitude. A memoised pass makes the whole range cost about as much as one long walk.

     

      Why it's fascinating

      The rule is small enough to teach a child, yet nobody can prove every number reaches 1 — Paul Erdős said "mathematics may not be ready for such problems." It has been checked by computer for every number up to roughly 2⁶⁸, and all of them fall. The parity of a number is just its last binary bit, so the whole map is secretly a statement about binary digits: even numbers shed a trailing zero, odd numbers get shuffled upward. The plot above shows the trademark hailstone motion — repeated climbs and plunges — before the inevitable collapse to the 4 → 2 → 1 loop.
